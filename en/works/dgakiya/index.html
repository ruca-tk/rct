01

Context

DG Camp Akiya is a newly opened incubation centre in Yokosuka — a fully renovated space for Digital Garage affiliates, accelerator startups, and global partners to cowork, hold events, and connect. The project began with a meeting: I was shown the space, given a logo, and handed some charts. From there, I had the creative freedom to define the concept, narrative, and full site experience. It was a high-visibility launch, with messages from a Diet member, the Mayor of Yokosuka, and the Mayor of Shibuya.

02

Direction

Yokosuka sits on the ocean. The space is designed for startups with global ambitions working from a local base. That tension — local roots, global reach — became the creative thread. I wrote the tagline: think globally, work locally. Everything else followed from it.

The visual language needed to feel dynamic and alive — reflecting the growth and energy of the startups the space was built to serve. Scale-based scroll animations, where elements grow and shrink as you move through the page, felt right for a space about momentum. I had been keeping a reference for the right project. This was it.

03

Key Decisions

Five decisions shaped the site beyond the original brief.

01. Drone Shot Concept I directed the drone to open from inside the conference room and pull back out over the ocean. It wasn't just an establishing shot — it was the tagline made visual. A startup working in this room, looking out at the sea, connected to markets beyond Japan. The concept and the footage were written together. Both were green-lit immediately.

02. Expanding the Brief With creative freedom over the scope, I added what the site needed to tell the full story: a concept section to communicate the vision, an about section to explain the facility's place within Digital Garage's global incubation network, and a topics section covering future plans. I worked with the team to gather detail on each initiative, sourced visuals to match, and structured them into three concrete cards: Cybersecurity Hub, Yokosuka City collaboration, and the Ryuichi Sakamoto Artist-in-Residence Studio.

03. Gallery with Editorial Labels Rather than presenting the property photography as a neutral grid, I organised it into four editorial categories — Dream, Create, Colab, Inspire — each with its own image set. I briefed the photographer on-site, section by section. The labels give the gallery a narrative arc that reflects what the space is actually for.

04. Kanagawa Wave Reference For the access section, I added a visual treatment referencing the wave art tradition associated with Kanagawa — the region where Yokosuka sits. It's a quiet cultural nod to the location, grounding the site locally without being decorative for its own sake.

05. Sakamoto Studio Presentation The Artist-in-Residence Studio is built around the legacy instruments and studio equipment of Ryuichi Sakamoto. The content was provided. Presenting it required careful treatment — significant enough to honour the legacy, restrained enough not to overshadow the facility itself.

04

Design

The site leads with the drone video hero and the tagline — no explanation needed before you've seen the ocean. The gallery follows immediately, letting the space sell itself through photography I directed on-site. The vision, hub, and studio sections build the story progressively: what the space is, where it sits in the global network, and what makes it culturally distinctive.

The motion language runs throughout — images and text scaling in and out as you scroll, giving the page a sense of energy that keeps pace with the space it represents. Dynamic and expressive, with each section revealing itself as you move through it.

05

Build

Built with Vite, Tailwind CSS, GSAP, Lenis, and vanilla JS — the same stack I rely on for motion-heavy single-page sites. GSAP handles the scale-based scroll animations throughout. Lenis keeps the scroll behaviour smooth and consistent. No framework overhead — full control over every interaction.

06

Outcome

The site launched alongside the facility opening and served as the primary announcement vehicle. The drone video and tagline were adopted without revision. The concept, about, and topics sections — none of which were in the original brief — became central parts of how the space is communicated publicly.

07

Reflection

The motion design reference I used was a video I had been saving for the right project. I knew the style before I knew the project. In hindsight I'd approach it the other way — let the content define the motion language rather than arriving with a reference already in hand. The scale animations work well here, but the fit was partly luck.
